CHAPTER 01

Why evaluate an offer at all

Most people spend weeks polishing a resume and rehearsing interview answers, then spend about ten minutes deciding whether to actually accept the job. That's backwards. The resume gets you the offer. The offer is the thing you'll actually live with — the paycheck, the manager, the hours, the fine print — for however long you stay.

A job offer is not just a "yes or no." It's a document — and a set of unwritten signals — that tells you a lot about a company, if you know where to look. This guide walks through four places to look: the salary, the job post itself, the contract, and the interview process, plus the questions that tend to surface what a company won't say outright.

Red flags rarely show up as one obvious lie. They show up as small inconsistencies — a vague answer here, a missing clause there — that only look serious once you line them up next to each other. This guide gives you the lineup.

None of this is about being paranoid or killing every offer with overanalysis. Most offers are fine. The point is to spend one focused hour checking, so you're not spending the next two years finding out the hard way.

CHAPTER 02

Is the salary actually fair?

"Market rate" gets thrown around constantly, but most people never check it — they compare the offer to their last salary, or a number a friend mentioned once. Neither tells you what the role is worth today, in your city, at your level.

Where to get real numbers

  • Levels.fyi / Glassdoor — strongest for tech and corporate roles; filter by title and location, not just company.
  • LinkedIn Salary / posts — useful for a general range, weaker for precision.
  • Industry communities — Slack groups, Discord servers, subreddits for your field often have blunt, current numbers.
  • Your own network — two or three direct messages to people in similar roles usually beats any salary site.

Aim for 3–5 data points, not one. A single number could be an outlier in either direction.

What actually moves a "fair" number

FactorWhy it matters
Years of relevant experienceNot total years worked — years doing this specific kind of work.
City / remote policyA remote role at a SF-based company may still pay SF rates, or may not — always confirm.
Company size & funding stageEarly-stage startups often pay less cash but more equity; established companies the reverse.
IndustryFinance and tech generally pay above average for equivalent seniority; nonprofits and education generally below.

CHAPTER 03

Red flags in the job post

The job post is written by the company, on the company's best day. If something is already vague or overselling at this stage, that tends to compound later, not resolve itself.

A vague title or duties list that could describe three different jobsUsually means the company hasn't defined the role yet — and you'll be the one defining it, unpaid, through trial and error.
"Fast-paced" and "like a family" standing in for an actual description of the day-to-dayNot automatically bad — but if these phrases are doing all the work in the post, ask what they're standing in for.
A huge posted salary range (e.g. $50K–$120K) with "competitive" languageWide ranges aren't inherently dishonest — but don't assume you'll land near the top without asking directly.
The same role gets reposted every few monthsCould mean rapid growth — or it could mean the role keeps turning over. Worth asking about directly.

None of these are automatic disqualifiers. They're prompts — things to bring up in the interview rather than assume the best or worst case.

CHAPTER 04

Red flags in the contract

This is the part almost everyone skims. A contract is the one document in this whole process that's actually enforceable, so it deserves more attention than the offer email that came with it.

No written probation period or notice periodIf it's not written down, it's not agreed — regardless of what was said out loud.
An overly broad non-compete relative to your actual role and seniorityA junior marketing hire generally shouldn't sign the same non-compete as a VP of Sales.
Benefits promised verbally but missing from the contractInsurance, bonus structure, remote flexibility — if it mattered enough to mention, it should matter enough to write down.
Undefined working hours or overtime policy"Flexible hours" without structure can mean genuine flexibility — or unpaid overtime with extra steps.
Vague payment terms — no clear date, currency, or methodEspecially relevant for remote or cross-border roles.

Simple move: before signing, ask "Can I get 24–48 hours to read this properly?" A reasonable employer will say yes without hesitation. Hesitation itself is information.

CHAPTER 05

Red flags in the interview itself

How a company runs its interview process is a preview of how it runs everything else. Sloppy, rushed, or evasive interviews rarely turn into calm, well-organized workplaces.

Pressure to accept within 24–48 hours, especially after a weeks-long processThat mismatch — slow process, sudden urgency — is worth noticing.
Deflection when you ask "why is this role open?"That's a completely normal question. A normal company can answer it directly.
You never meet your actual manager or team before acceptingMeeting only HR or a recruiter throughout the process is a gap worth flagging.
Every culture question gets the same scripted answer, word for wordConsistent messaging is fine. Identical rehearsed lines from multiple people is a different thing.

One or two of these alone might just be a busy hiring process. Several together, stacked on contract or salary concerns, is the pattern worth paying attention to.

CHAPTER 06

Questions worth asking before you accept

These are designed to sound completely normal in an interview — because they are — while actually surfacing useful information.

Why is this role open — is it growth, or did someone leave?
A direct, specific answer is a good sign. A vague or uncomfortable answer is worth noting.
What does success look like at 30/60/90 days?
If nobody can answer this, the role likely isn't well-defined yet — see chapter 03.
How many people have held this role in the last two years?
A direct way to ask about turnover without asking about turnover.
How are raises and promotions actually decided here?
Listen for a real process versus "it depends" with no further detail.
What does the team's current workload look like, realistically?
Good managers can answer this honestly. Evasive answers here often predict burnout later.

Asking sharp, specific questions doesn't make you look difficult — it's usually read as a sign that you take the role seriously. Companies worth working for tend to welcome it.

The final checklist

If you can honestly check every box below, you're in good shape to sign.

I compared the salary to 3+ real data points, not gut feel or a single site
I read the full contract myself, not just the offer email or summary
Probation period, notice period, and working hours are written down clearly
I asked why the role is open, and the answer made sense
I spoke with my future manager directly, not only HR or a recruiter
